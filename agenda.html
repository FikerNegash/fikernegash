Research agenda

I study the political economy of development, with a focus on public service delivery in low-capacity contexts, especially in urban sub-Saharan Africa. I want to understand what drives both weak and strong public services, and in particular how civil service reform and technology adoption shape their quality.

What draws me to this is how quickly broad questions about the state become personal and specific. Where elections do not settle much, the state a citizen first meets is usually a service provider in the bureaucracy: a clinic, a school, a council office. My research asks how people learn about citizenship from these encounters, how they make demands of the state, when they provide for themselves instead, and what keeps the state from responding.

This work unfolds across two sets of questions:

  1. Barriers to responsive public services: why local bureaucrats do not act on what citizens want, even when told.
  2. The deep roots of state trajectories in Africa: how built spaces, climate, and colonial legacies shaped where countries ended up.

The first is a working paper with Daniel Agness, Pascaline Dupas, and Tigabu Getahun. We test whether giving local bureaucrats in urban Ethiopia information on public service delivery changes their knowledge, priorities, and effort. I joined after fieldwork had ended and led the analysis of the survey and line-item budget data. The project has deepened my interest in bureaucratic capacity and autonomy, particularly in cities.

The second is a longer-term interest. As an Ethiopian and an observer of politics there and in a few other countries, I want to understand the historical and geographical factors behind why some countries have fared differently from others. A first step is a note on built spaces: in Madhya Pradesh, India, I use new rural roads to ask whether more passing trips past a village council office change how much work households ask for under the rural jobs programme.

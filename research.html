Research agenda

My research agenda is motivated by an awareness of how easily broad questions about the state and politics become immediately personal and specific. For this reason, I am interested in the politics of how citizens first encounter the state, which in non-electoral contexts often takes the form of public service providers in the bureaucracy. How do individuals learn about citizenship from the state? How do they make demands of it, and when do they provide for themselves instead? What are the barriers that keep the state unresponsive? These are the questions my research seeks to answer.

A related set of interests has to do with trends in African countries. As an Ethiopian and an observer of politics there and in a few other countries, I am interested in the historical and geographical factors that may lie behind the different socioeconomic trajectories of some countries relative to others. As part of these interests, I want to explore built spaces, the role of climate, and colonial legacies in shaping modern-day states.

As part of this agenda, I have a working paper on the informational and motivational barriers to state responsiveness in Ethiopia. The paper tests whether providing information on public service delivery to local bureaucrats in urban Ethiopia affects their knowledge, priorities, and effort. Although I joined the project after fieldwork had concluded, I led the analysis of the survey and line-item budget data to assess how information could shift alignment with public priorities. This project has deepened my interest in bureaucratic capacity and autonomy, particularly in urban settings.
